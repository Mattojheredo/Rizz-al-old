The Works of Rizal

A doctor by training and a writer by calling, Rizal turned his observations into stories that colonial authorities could not ignore. Browse his major novels, poems, and essays, with summaries of the plots, the historical background, and the lessons each one carries.

Major Literary Works

❦
Noli Me Tángere

Rizal Exposes the Friars in a Novel

Rizal’s first novel, published in 1887. It follows Crisóstomo Ibarra, who returns from Europe and runs into the abuses of the Spanish friars and colonial officials.

Background of the Story

Rizal wrote Noli Me Tángere in Europe and had it printed in Berlin in 1887. The story is set in the fictional town of San Diego, where Crisóstomo Ibarra comes home after seven years abroad and wants to build a school. The friars and officials stand in his way, and the novel shows how abuse of power destroys one man’s hope for peaceful reform.

Major Characters

Crisóstomo IbarraMain character
A wealthy young Filipino who studied in Europe for seven years. He is idealistic and believes in education and peaceful reform. His father, Don Rafael, died in disgrace, and Ibarra spends the novel trying to build a school and clear his family’s name.
María ClaraIbarra’s beloved
The sweet and devoted daughter of Kapitan Tiago, and Ibarra’s fiancée. She is the model of the obedient young woman of her time, but she is forced to choose between love and the secret of her birth.
ElíasThe fugitive
A boatman and fugitive who saves Ibarra’s life twice. He believes in revolution for justice, while Ibarra believes in reform, and the two men’s talks show the two paths open to the country.
Padre DamasoThe arrogant friar
A proud Franciscan who was once the parish priest of San Diego. He had a long grudge against Don Rafael Ibarra, and he stands for the abuse of power by the clergy.
Padre SalvíThe scheming curate
The soft-spoken friar who replaces Padre Damaso as curate of San Diego. He secretly desires María Clara and uses his power to set Ibarra up.
SisaA mother who loses everything
A poor and loving mother of Basilio and Crispin. She is driven mad after her sons are taken from her, and her story shows the suffering of ordinary people.
Basilio and CrispinSisa’s sons
Two young brothers who work as sacristans. Crispin is falsely accused of stealing, and Basilio’s story continues into El Filibusterismo.
Pilósopo TasyoThe wise old man
A thoughtful elder whom the town calls crazy because his ideas are ahead of his time. He gives Ibarra advice and warns him about the danger he is in.
Kapitan TiagoThe wealthy cabeza
A rich man who stays close to the friars and the Spanish officials to protect his money and position, and the legal father of María Clara.
Doña VictorinaThe social climber
A Filipina who pretends to be Spanish and looks down on her own people. Rizal uses her to make fun of those who copy the colonizers.
El Filibusterismo

The Sequel Where Things Get Darker

The 1891 sequel. Ibarra returns in disguise as the jeweler Simoun, and the story turns from reform to revenge.

Background of the Story

El Filibusterismo takes place about thirteen years after Noli Me Tángere and was printed in Ghent in 1891. Simoun, a rich and feared jeweler, is really Ibarra in disguise, and he has come back for revenge on the system that ruined him. Meanwhile, Basilio is now a medical student and a group of students fights for a school that teaches Spanish. The novel asks whether violence can fix an unjust society.

Major Characters

SimounThe jeweler
Ibarra in disguise. He is rich, feared, and bitter, and he uses his wealth to push the colonial government into abuses so the people will finally revolt. His plan shows how hatred can twist an idealist.
BasilioThe medical student
Sisa’s son, now a hardworking student near the end of his medical course. He wants to finish his studies and stay out of politics, but injustice keeps pulling him in.
IsaganiThe poet
A passionate student and Paulita’s sweetheart. He is the idealist of the new generation, and he believes in the cause but not in violence.
Padre FlorentinoThe wise priest
Isagani’s uncle and a good and thoughtful priest. He represents faith and conscience, and Simoun spends his last hours with him.
Kabesang TalesThe farmer who loses everything
A farmer who loses his land to the friars and his daughter to their schemes. He later joins the outlaws, and his story shows how injustice pushes ordinary people to extremes.
JuliBasilio’s sweetheart
Kabesang Tales’s daughter. She works to free her father and is put in a terrible position by the friars, which becomes one of the most tragic parts of the novel.
Padre CamorraThe abusive friar
A friar who abuses his position. He stands for the corrupt clergy that Rizal criticizes.
Don CustodioThe lazy official
A government adviser who makes decisions based on his own convenience. Rizal uses him to show how weak and corrupt the officials were.
Paulita GómezIsagani’s sweetheart
A pretty and wealthy girl who in the end chooses to marry Juanito Pelaez.
Juanito PelaezThe social climber
A student who flatters the teachers and the friars to get ahead, and who marries Paulita.
Mi Último Adiós

The Farewell Poem Rizal Left Behind

The poem Rizal wrote the night before his execution on December 30, 1896. It’s his farewell to the country he loved.

Background of the Story

Rizal wrote Mi Último Adiós in Fort Santiago in his last hours, before he was shot at Bagumbayan on December 30, 1896. The poem is in Spanish, with fourteen stanzas of five lines each, and it had no title until after his death. The story passed down says he hid it inside an alcohol stove and gave it to his sister Trinidad.

Who Speaks in the Poem

Mi Último Adiós is a poem, so it has no characters like the two novels. The speaker is Rizal himself, and he is speaking to the Philippines, which he calls his adored homeland and the “Pearl of the Orient Sea.” He also speaks to his parents, his friends, and the people he loves.

The speakerRizal himself
A man who has accepted that he will die and wants to say goodbye. He is calm, not angry, and says he gives his life gladly for his country.
The homelandWhom the poem is addressed to
The Philippines, which he calls his beloved homeland. The whole poem is a farewell and a promise of love to the country.
Family and friendsThe people he leaves behind
He speaks to his parents, his friends, and the people he loved. These lines show the human side of his sacrifice.
Those who fall in the struggleThe fallen
He mentions those who died fighting for freedom, and says he joins them, which makes his death part of something larger than himself.

Writing Timeline

  • 1887 Noli Me Tángere, printed in Berlin
  • 1891 El Filibusterismo, printed in Ghent
  • 1896 Mi Último Adiós, written before his execution

Key Characters

  • Crisóstomo Ibarra: the idealist who returns from Europe
  • María Clara: Ibarra's beloved
  • Elías: the fugitive who helps Ibarra
  • Simoun: Ibarra in disguise in the sequel
  • Basilio: the student whose story ties both novels together

Did You Know?

“Noli me tangere” is Latin for “touch me not.” Rizal took the phrase from the Gospel of John, and used it to describe a society's wounds that no one dared to touch.
